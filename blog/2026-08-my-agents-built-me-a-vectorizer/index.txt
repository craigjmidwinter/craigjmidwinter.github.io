Every time I want a crisp SVG out of a picture-- a sticker, a logo, a file for a cutter-- I end up on some website that wants the image on their server and my card on file. A credit per conversion, a watermark until you pay, a queue when they're busy. And the actual job is math. It's edge detection and curve fitting. It should run on my machine, for free, forever.

So this is the post where the [pit crew loop](/blog/2026-08-the-gauntlet-loop-needs-a-pit-crew/) stops racing karts and does a day of honest work. [GetVect](https://getvect.midwinter.io) is a local desktop vectorizer for macOS: drop in a PNG, JPEG or BMP, get back SVG, EPS, DXF, PDF or PNG from an offline tracing engine. No account, no credits, offline by default. It's [MIT-licensed on GitHub](https://github.com/craigjmidwinter/getvect), v0.1.0 has a real dmg on the releases page, and the mascot is Frankie, my actual orange tabby, traced by his own app.

The app is the deliverable, but it's not the story. The story is that the first commit and the v0.1.0 tag are 23 hours and 47 minutes apart, 119 commits, and the loop that built it spent a meaningful chunk of that time catching itself-- which is exactly what the pit crew thesis said should happen.

**What did I actually ask for?**

Feature parity with the raster-to-vector workflow of a leading online vectorizer, which I'm going to keep politely unnamed, for reasons that'll become obvious when I tell you what got checked into the test fixtures. Same loop as the kart experiment: builders implement a slice at a time (engine, then shell, then settings, then export), fresh-context critics score the running app against the reference, and a pit crew does nothing but build measurement-- a Playwright-for-Electron harness, a fixture generator, and a "light meter" that rasterizes every output SVG back to pixels and diffs it against the source. Fix laps run until the integration critic clears 8.5, capped at five laps.

The first thing that broke had nothing to do with agents: macOS XProtect decided the Electron dev binary was malware and deleted it out of node_modules. Twice, including the re-download. The pit crew had pinned an end-of-life Electron; current Electron launches clean, and there's now a postinstall hook that de-quarantines the binary, because an environment fix that lives in one shell session is not a fix.

**How do you write a bar that isn't adjectives?**

The original spec was written from the reference product's marketing copy, and that's how these loops die: "matches the reference" doesn't fail CI any more than "make it amazing" does. The correction was the best decision of the whole build. An agent drove the real product live in Chrome-- it processes signed-out, and its result pane turns out to be an inline SVG element, so every settings combination is one DOM read away from being an exemplar. Its actual outputs, at fully-known settings, are now checked into `fixtures/reference/` and the harness measures our engine against them on every run, including where the reference wins.

The recon rewrote the spec on the spot. The settings model I'd guessed-- sliders for detail, smoothing, despeckle-- isn't how the product thinks at all. It thinks in model presets and candidate palettes and per-colour output groups. And it surfaced the single number the whole engine now revolves around: at otherwise identical settings, turning on Smart anti-aliasing collapsed 354 paths to 67. That's 81% fewer paths *and* smoother output, replicated on two more subjects (637 to 63, 758 to 41). It's a pre-trace edge cleanup, not a rendering garnish, and it's the difference between output that looks traced and output that looks drawn.

That's the first pattern worth stealing: don't grade a clone against your memory of the product. Go get the product's actual homework and staple it into CI.

**Where did the loop cheat?**

My favourite failure of the run, because it's the [220 assertions](/blog/2026-08-220-assertions-and-a-thumb/) lesson wearing a different hat. For an entire lap, the fidelity instruments decoded test images by flattening transparency onto white. The app's own decode path handed the engine transparent pixels as (0,0,0,0)-- which the engine, never having been taught about alpha, read as opaque black. So every transparent-background PNG, the entire sticker-and-decal use case this product exists for, was tracing an invented black background. And the instruments scored it green, because they were measuring a picture the app could never produce. That one scared the shit out of me.

The fix is one of my favourite commit messages in the repo-- "transparency is a group with no colour"-- and the durable fix is a decode-parity spec whose only job is to go red the moment the app and the harness disagree about what a pixel is. A number measured on pixels the product never sees is not a measurement of the product.

**What did fresh-context critics actually buy?**

The lap-5 critic did the thing fresh eyes are for: it invented a sharper instrument than any we'd built. Feed the engine the exact palette it just returned-- a no-op edit, by definition-- and the geometry changed. Every existing palette spec passed because they all ran on a flat logo fixture where the identity happens to hold. The critic's score *dropped* the app from 8.0 to 7.5 that lap, and it's the healthiest data point on the chart: nothing regressed, somebody measured something nobody had thought to measure.

Then it kept happening, in a way I've started to trust: three briefs in a row were corrected by their own builders, by measurement, before a line of fix was written. I said the cheek stripes were being starved in curve fitting; the agent proved they vanished with smoothing at zero. The actual culprit was a colour-distance test computed in L1:

```js
da + db <= span * 1.35
```

Three characters of wrongness. L1 distance is additive along any monotone path through the colour cube, so with black as one neighbour-- that is, next to any outline, which is every drawing this app is for-- that test classifies whole features as "just a blend" and deletes them before palette selection even runs. The fox fixture's cyan eyes: 1,071 pixels in, 7 out. After the fix they come back at 93%-- and the reference product's own output scores 0% on the same instrument. On the defect class we found by measuring ourselves, we now beat the product we cloned.

Same shape a third time: I looked at an outline at high zoom and said the steps "read as pixels." The agent killed that diagnosis with one table-- the boundary entering the curve fitter was already smooth to 0.06 px-- and showed the fitter was putting the staircase back, sagging 0.72 px mid-segment while staying inside its error budget. The judge that settled it is a fixture of mathematically exact circles, gated on deviation from the equation: worst arc 0.424 px before, 0.229 after. The pattern, explicitly: your diagnosis is a hypothesis, the instrument is the judge, and a builder who measures before fixing beats an orchestrator who guessed.

**So is it actually any good?**

On every number we know how to compute, yes. Against the reference's own export of the same art: mean colour error 5.96 vs their 13.50, curve-command ratio 0.986 vs 0.639, smaller file. The demo on the site is Frankie going from a 568 KB photo-derived PNG to a 19 KB SVG in 59 shapes and 7 colour layers, transparent background intact; the same source through the reference comes back at 21.7 KB in 40 paths. Fewer shapes than ours, in more bytes-- both halves of that sentence are checked in and re-measured on every run, so I'd be publishing them the same way if they pointed the other way.

And then the last critic conceded every number and zoomed to 100% on the mouth, where the reference draws two clean white fangs and one continuous arc, and we drew cream holes ringed by ink and a jaw line that turned teal for 21% of its length. The loop retired at 8.3 against a bar of 8.5, with four named, mechanical fixes on the table (a final surgical round applied them before release). The honest summary of seven laps: parity everywhere the instruments reach, and taste living in the last half point. I don't think that half point is unreachable-- I think nobody has built the instrument that measures it yet, and I notice that every time I say that about something, somebody's fresh-context critic eventually does.

Two more honesty notes before the download link. Flat sticker art like Frankie is favourable input for a tracer-- hard edges, few colours, nothing to guess at-- and the fixture population is still narrow: two stickers and some synthetics, no typography, no photographs. The gates are real, but the corpus they're calibrated on is one cat deep. That's written in the repo, not just here.

**Their best trick wasn't tracing at all**

Halfway through, someone asked why our output looked so much worse than the reference on a shaded character, and the answer rearranged my whole mental model: their "Enhance with AI" is not a filter. It's a generative image-to-image re-illustration-- background deleted, airbrushed shading flattened into bands, outlines redrawn-- and then their tracer traces the already-flat art. The moat was never the vectorizer. It was the model standing in front of it.

GetVect's answer is bring-your-own-key: paste a Gemini API key, flip the switch, and the enhanced image becomes what the engine traces. One call, about 8 seconds. But the trade gets stated in plain text right at the toggle: with it on, *that image is uploaded to Google*-- the exact thing every other part of this app exists to avoid. It ships off, the key lives encrypted in the Keychain and is never returned to the UI, and a failed call falls back to tracing the un-enhanced image and says why. The fully-local version-- background removal plus an on-device flatten, nothing leaving the machine-- is [issue #1](https://github.com/craigjmidwinter/getvect/issues/1), and the provider layer was written so it arrives as a plug-in, not a rewrite.

**The jank, named before you find it**

The build is unsigned, so macOS quarantines the dmg and claims it "cannot be opened"-- nobody has paid Apple to vouch for it yet. Right-click, Open, or one `xattr` command; the README walks through it. The update story is a banner, not an updater: GetVect asks GitHub once per launch if there's a newer version and shows a dismissible link, because macOS won't let an unsigned app replace itself in place-- the silent updater is written and dormant until a signing cert exists. Apple Silicon only for now; Intel, Windows and Linux build from source but aren't tested, so they aren't offered as if they were. And at high zoom, sharp corners still render rounder than they should-- 75 degrees against a 112-degree target-- because the lever that fixes corners pushes two other fidelity gates over their bars, and a ratchet you loosen to claim a win is not a ratchet.

**Loose ends**

The local Enhance provider is the big one. After that: a corpus sweep, because the engine needs to meet typography and photographs before I trust the gates as general; and the corner problem wants a real fix, not a loosened gate. The whole build is chronicled as it happened in [the repo's devlog](https://github.com/craigjmidwinter/getvect/tree/main/katra/entries), every entry stamped with the commits it describes, if you want the unedited version of everything above.

Mostly though: [go download it](https://github.com/craigjmidwinter/getvect/releases/latest), feed it something that isn't a cat sticker, and tell me where it falls over. That's the instrument I can't build-- your art, your squint. [Issues are open](https://github.com/craigjmidwinter/getvect/issues), and the harness means a good bug report tends to become a permanent gate within the day.
